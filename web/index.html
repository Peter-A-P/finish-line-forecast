Project 12: Finish Line Forecast

Predictions published before the gun, scored after it

How fast will you run on race day? Written down before the gun, and checked after.

Every registered runner at a Newfoundland road race gets a predicted finish time, a range it should land in, and a likely place, worked out from eighteen years of public race results. Each prediction is a blend of two models, because the two make different mistakes and the average of them is more accurate than either one. The predictions are locked in a public record before the race starts, so nobody can quietly adjust them afterwards, and once the official results are posted the error is published right beside them. A race director can plan pacers, the finish-line clock and medical staff from expected finishing times rather than guesses, and a runner gets a goal time with an honest range instead of a hunch.

The two models are a hierarchical Bayesian model and LightGBM gradient-boosted trees, averaged {{blend_parent_weight}} to {{blend_weight}} on the log scale. Point at either name for what it means, and Does it work? has both measured against three rules of thumb.

The races and their predictions

Pick a race. From seven days before it, every runner on the entrant list gets a prediction the first morning they appear; the day before, the whole field is predicted again with the latest weather forecast and given places. Until then, you can see how the same model would have done at this race last year, and what the course and its history look like.

How it was built, and how it works

From a folder of old results pages to a prediction you can hold it to

The rest of this page is the whole method, in the order it happens. Each part starts in plain language, with a picture. Where there is more to say for someone who builds models for a living, it is folded away under For the technical reader, so a reader who only wants the idea can skip it and a reader who wants the detail loses nothing.

The whole idea in one picture

Seven steps, and the order matters: the prediction is fixed in public before the race exists, so the scoring at the end is a real test rather than a story told afterwards.

  1. 1ReadEvery public road-race result in the province since {{first_year}}, fetched once, politely.
  2. 2ResolveJoin results into people, with no ID number to go on.
  3. 3AdjustPut every race on one scale: distance, course, heat, wind.
  4. 4ModelEstimate each runner's fitness, borrowing strength from similar runners, and average that with a second model that learns differently.
  5. 5PredictA time, an honest range and a likely place for everyone registered.
  6. 6LockCommit, fingerprint and date-stamp the prediction in public before the gun.
  7. 7ScoreCompare against the official results, and publish the error.

Most predictions of this kind are never checked. A backtest, where a model predicts races that have already happened, is useful and is done here too, but it is always possible to tune a model until its backtest looks good. A prediction locked before the race cannot be tuned afterwards. That is the point of the whole project.

Eighteen years of results

The Newfoundland and Labrador Athletics Association has published the results of nearly every road race in the province for decades, as plain text pages. This project reads {{races}} of them: {{finishes}} finishes by {{runners}} runners over {{courses}} courses. There are no results for 2020, when the races did not run.

Before any of it was read, the two organisations whose pages it uses were told what would be fetched and why, and offered the chance to say no. That is not only a courtesy: the crawler refuses to run until it has been told the notes went out, and the automated checks confirm that it refuses. It fetches one page a second, each page once, and keeps it.

For the technical reader: parsing pages with no column contract

The pages are fixed-width text inside a <pre> block, in three layouts across the years, and two thirds of them draw no ruler under the header. Column boundaries are measured from the character positions that are blank in every row, and header words are assigned to a column rather than sliced by it, because several pages centre their headers left of their data. An unrecognised header raises and names itself rather than landing values in the wrong fields; {{unparsed}} pages still refuse, and each is listed in the repository.

Two findings from reading it: eight years of the archive were silently missing because older index pages say "Road Race Series" where newer ones say "Road Running", and eighty apparent courses were really {{courses}}, because older pages name a race after its sponsor of the year. Both are regression tests now.

Working out who is who

Results pages print a name, and sometimes an age group and a hometown. There is no runner ID. So before anything can be predicted, the results have to be joined into people, and the obvious way to do it turns out to be wrong.

The obvious rule: same name and same town

Runners move, and most pages print no hometown at all. Of the 1,656 names printed under two or more towns, 1,103 show one clean switch over time, which is what moving house looks like. Splitting on the town turned one runner into two people with half a history each.

The rule used: nobody gets younger

Every age group printed on a dated race implies a range of birth years, and one person's ranges must overlap. That is the only thing allowed to split a name. The hometown only breaks a tie.

When in doubt, it refuses. Splitting one runner in two costs some accuracy. Merging two people into one would publish a confident prediction for a real person built from somebody else's results. So {{ambiguous}} runners the archive cannot tell apart are left out of every published prediction and counted, rather than guessed at.

For the technical reader: what that trade costs, and how it is bounded

Names are compared on a normalised key (case, accents, apostrophes, spacing, hyphens), never fuzzily: nicknames are deliberately not merged, because Mike and Michael Power are often two people. Within a name key, results are clustered by intersecting birth-year windows; a result compatible with more than one cluster and carrying no age group is held back. Allowing towns to change means two people of one name and a compatible age can merge. That risk is published rather than argued about: the share of resolved runners whose printed town flips back and forth, the pattern two merged people would make, is reported in the README, and reading them shows most are one person spelling their own town differently.

For a live race the same rules link each person on the entrant list to at most one history, using the list's sex, and its hometown where it prints one, to break ties.

A 5 km and a marathon on one scale

A runner's history is a mix of distances. To compare them, every time is converted to a single fitness number using Jack Daniels' running tables, which runners know as VDOT. Two performances with the same VDOT are equally good, whatever the distance.

The model then works on one quantity: how much slower or faster than a reference runner each finish was, as a percentage. That puts every race in the archive on one scale before anything is estimated. What is left for the model is what the tables cannot know: this runner, this road, this year and this morning.

For the technical reader: the response variable

The model's outcome is log(time / Daniels time for VDOT 50 at that distance). On the log scale 0.05 is five percent of a finish time wherever it was run, errors are roughly symmetric, and the population's fade over distance is Daniels' curve. Each runner's own fade departs from it by a coefficient that shrinks to zero for anyone who has raced only one distance. A population-wide departure is not estimated at all: every course is run at one distance, so it cannot be told apart from the course effects, and fitting both left it unidentified. It is why the course chart further down is read in a column per race length rather than across.

Some roads are slower than others

Cape to Cabot climbs Signal Hill at the finish; Turkey Tea runs mostly downhill. Runners cross from course to course, so each course's difficulty can be measured from the results themselves: the same people, on different roads. Each dot is a course; the line through it is how sure the measurement is.

Read down a column, not across the chart. Every finish is scored against a reference time for its own distance, and this population fades over the long distances a little harder than the reference expects. That departure has nowhere to go but the courses that are long, so the tall columns on the right are partly a race length and not a road. Two courses in the same column are two roads, measured on the same runners, and that comparison is clean.

The Tely 10 is a fast road, and only its own column says so. Against the flat reference it reads {{tely_factor}}, which looks like any other course. Measured against its own length it is {{tely_peers}}, and that is the comparison with a road in it: the Tely drops more than it climbs and the prevailing westerly is behind the field. Both figures come out of the same fit. The first still has the race length inside it and the second does not. One caveat kept in view: at 10 miles this archive has one other course, the hilly Bell Island Blast, so that is two roads compared and not ten.

The measurement agrees with the physics, without being tuned to it. Cape to Cabot measures about {{c2c_factor}} slower than flat. Run that number backwards through the energy cost of running uphill (Minetti's curve) and the published 550 m of climb implies an average grade of about 10 percent on the hills. The race's own course page says "grades of more than 10 per cent in some parts". Two independent routes to one number, and the agreement is a ballpark rather than a decimal: at 20 km it is the only course on the archive, so a point or so of that {{c2c_factor}} is the same race-length effect, and the grade that explains the rest is nearer 8 to 10 percent.

For the technical reader: separating the course from the runners on it, and the length from the road

What the reference costs. The outcome is a finish over Daniels' time for a reference runner at that distance, and each runner carries one level for their whole career, so a population that fades over distance differently from Daniels' curve leaves that difference in the effects of the long courses. The archive shows it plainly: {{marathon_span}} Measured against each other the marathons scatter around zero, which is what five ordinary road marathons should do. Course and length cannot be separated from finishes alone, because every course is run at one distance, so both numbers are published and neither is dressed up as the other. The Tely read from runners who also race a half marathon or longer is 1.3 points faster than the same course read from runners who never go beyond 15 km, which is the mechanism caught in the act. None of this reaches a prediction: a course factor is only ever applied to that course, at its own distance, where the length belongs in the answer.

Course and edition effects are estimated jointly with per-runner levels and trends by alternating least squares on the log-ratio scale, with a bootstrap over runners for the intervals; the full model then carries a zero-sum course effect with editions summing to zero within their course. Leaving out the per-runner trend produced a striking false finding: every well-covered course appeared to get harder by about 0.6% a year. It was the field ageing, absorbed into the course. With the trend in, the drift is zero and the signs scatter, and a test plants ageing on unchanged courses to keep it that way.

What the weather costs

A warm, sunny morning slows a field, and it costs a marathoner far more than a 5 km runner, who is only out in it for twenty minutes. The model reads the hourly weather at St. John's airport for the hours each race was actually running, and learns what heat and wind cost.

Weather moves a whole field by a few percent, and that is easy to miss. A few percent of a finish time is small next to how much one person varies from race to race, so adding weather barely changes the average error per runner, and an early version of this project wrongly concluded that weather did not matter here. Measured at the level of a whole race, it clearly does: without it, the model's errors grow with the heat; with it, they do not.

For the technical reader: a hinge on felt temperature, and why not a straight line

Heat enters the race effect as max(0, T + s x sun - 12 C) x (h0 + h1 log(d / 5 km)), where sun is the direct radiation over the race hours as a share of a clear noon and s, the degrees a full sun adds, is estimated (posterior median 2.1 C, 95% interval 0.1 to 9.2, from a prior on the US National Weather Service's 15 F). Both heat coefficients are constrained positive, so a hot morning never makes a race faster. Wind enters as a speed and, on point-to-point courses with a known bearing, as a signed tailwind.

The straight line in temperature that most race models use was built, backtested and rejected: on two leave-one-year-out tests over eighteen years it was the worst shape offered, and on 19,035 runners who ran consecutive editions of the same race it predicted the change between years worse than predicting no change at all. It also hid inside the calendar-year effect. The full write-up is the rejected approach.

For a live race the weather is a forecast, not an observation, so its error was measured on past race mornings at this airport for each lead time from one to seven days, and the forecast is corrected and spread by that error before it enters the prediction.

The model: borrowing strength

Most runners have only one or two results in the archive. One race is a thin basis for a prediction: it might have been a great day or a bad one. So the model does what a good coach does with a new athlete: start from what is typical for someone of that age and sex, and move toward the runner's own results as more of them arrive.

This is what "hierarchical" means. Every runner has their own fitness, but those fitness levels are estimated together, each pulled toward its group by exactly as much as its own evidence is weak. A runner with eight races is predicted almost entirely from them; a first-timer is predicted from people like them.

For the technical reader: the model, as written 
log(time / Daniels time) = alpha_i + beta_i log(d / 10 km) + walk_i[year]
                           + course[c] + year[t] + heat + wind + edition + noise

alpha_i      ~ Normal(mu_group[g_i], sigma_alpha)        fitness in the first year raced
beta_i       ~ Normal(0, sigma_beta)                     own fade over distance
walk_i[y]    = walk_i[y'] + Normal(mu_trend[g_i] gap,    form, carried between racing
                                   sigma_walk sqrt(gap))  years, drifting with age
course       ~ ZeroSumNormal(sigma_course)
year[t]      = year[t-1] + Normal(0, sigma_year)         what every race that year shared
noise        ~ StudentT(nu, 0, sigma_eps)                a bad day is not Gaussian

Fitted in PyMC with the nutpie sampler, non-centred throughout. PyMC's default NUTS hit its maximum tree depth with R-hat above 2 on this model; nutpie sampled the same model cleanly. A prediction is not a single number but the full set of posterior draws, each walked forward to race day and given a fresh morning and a fresh bad day; the median is the headline time and the quantiles feed the ranges below.

An honest limitation, stated in the repository beside the numbers: years since a runner's first race and the calendar year move together, so the group drift, the year effect and the group means trade off along a ridge the sampler mixes poorly on (worst R-hat 1.41 to 2.15 across fits). The quantities a returning runner's prediction reads converge (median R-hat 1.006); a first-timer's starting level does not fully, which is why first-timers' ranges are calibrated separately, and why a constraint that was tried to fix it, and made every chain disagree, was taken out.

The first version gave each runner a straight-line career trend. The backtest refuted it: carried to race day, a line fitted across a runner's improving early years predicts improvement nobody has, 8.3% too fast across 49 races. Form as a random walk replaced it.

Ranges that keep their promise

A prediction without a range is an opinion. Each runner gets a range that should contain their finish four times in five (80%) and a wider one for nine in ten (90%). The test of a range is simple: across many runners, does it hold as often as it claims?

The model's own ranges were a little too narrow for runners with one to three races, and calibration fixes that. Before each race, the ranges are widened or narrowed by exactly as much as they missed on earlier races, separately for each depth of history. A first-timer's range is wide, around {{width_new}} minutes, and a runner with a long history gets about {{width_deep}}: the range is as wide as the evidence requires and no wider.

The same test on the machine-learning challenger

LightGBM predicts a range directly, by fitting one model per edge of the range. Those raw ranges make no promise at all about how often they hold, and the chart shows what that means in practice. The same calibration step repairs them.

{{gbm_ranges}}

What the promise is, and is not. The calibration method, split conformal prediction, guarantees coverage on average over races within a group, provided a new race's errors look like earlier races' errors. It is not a promise about any one runner or any one race, and it will fail on a morning unlike any before it: a gale on Signal Hill is exactly that. This caveat is printed beside every coverage table this project publishes.

For the technical reader: Mondrian split conformal, rolled forward in time

Conformalised quantile regression on the log scale. Each scored runner's non-conformity score is how far outside the model's own interval their finish fell; a new race's interval is stretched multiplicatively by the finite-sample quantile of the scores from races dated strictly before it, per history-depth stratum (0, 1, 2 to 3, 4 or more prior results). Two races on the same morning are not each other's calibration. A stratum with fewer than fifty earlier scores is left unadjusted and counted, and coverage intervals resample races rather than runners, because runners in one race share its morning.

From times to places

A place depends on everyone else in the race. So the whole field is simulated together, thousands of times, and each runner's place is read off the simulations: a most likely place and a range.

One morning per simulated race

In each simulation every runner shares the same weather and the same course. A hot morning slows the whole field together and barely changes the order, and simulating each runner's morning separately would get that wrong.

Runners from away

At the biggest races, visitors with no results here reach the top ten. The model cannot know which newcomer is a visiting elite runner, so at those races newcomers are drawn from how past first-timers on that course actually finished, and the predicted top 20 shows the places they are expected to take as placeholders rather than handing them to a name.

For the technical reader: comparing placing fairly

A naive placing table ranks each model among the runners it answers for, which ranks "last time" only among runners with a history and the model over the whole field, newcomers included, whose order is close to a guess. That comparison made the model look twice as bad as it is. The fair comparison ranks both over exactly the runners both answered for, race by race, with a bootstrap over races for the interval; both tables are published, because scoring each model on the subset that suits it is how a table stops being checkable.

Does it work?

Every race from {{bt_first_year}} on was predicted from results dated strictly before it, as if it were tomorrow's race, and compared with what actually happened. The model has to beat three simple rules of thumb, which are always shown first, and a standard machine-learning challenger given the same information.

One thing to be clear about before the numbers: none of these rules of thumb were lying around. "You will run what you ran last time" sounds like something anybody could do, and for one runner with one race in front of them it is. For a field of several hundred it is not. It needs eighteen years of results pages read and parsed, the same person's results joined across those years with no runner ID to go on, their last race converted to this race's distance through Daniels' tables, and this course's difficulty measured against every other course in the province. All of that is this project, and the rules of thumb are computed by it. So the comparison below is not this model against something a runner can look up; it is this model against the best simple answer the same machinery can give, which is a harder test and the only honest one.

And a rule of thumb cannot answer for everybody. "Last time" has nothing to say about a runner with no past result, and about {{newcomer_share}} of a field is that runner: it answers for {{cf_answered}} of these entrants and a race calculator for {{vdot_answered}}, while the published model answers for {{model_answered}} and carries a measured error for the ones it is least sure about. A race director planning a finish-line clock needs the whole field, not the two thirds of it with a history.

For runners with a real history, the model is {{skill}} closer than "last time": {{mae_model}} minutes against {{mae_cf}}. For runners with one or two races the gain is small, which is expected: one result is one result. For first-timers every method is out by about {{mae_new}} minutes, because nothing about them is known but their age group. That, not the model comparison, is the real size of the problem, and it is why every prediction carries a range.

Is five minutes good? It depends what you are running

An average error in minutes is not one claim across distances. The same model that is out by a minute and a half over 5 km is out by a quarter of an hour over a marathon, and both are about the same fraction of the runner's day. So here it is both ways, for the whole field and for the runners the archive knows well. An average is also not a margin every prediction carries, so the middle of the misses and the ninth decile are beside it: half of these runners were predicted closer than the one and nine in ten closer than the other, while a handful, usually somebody having a very bad day or a very good one, are further out than any of these numbers.

For the technical reader: how two models become one prediction

Averaging is on the log scale, which is the scale everything here is measured on, and the weight sits on the trees: log(published) = {{blend_parent_weight}} log(Bayesian) + {{blend_weight}} log(trees). But a published prediction is not one number. It carries two ranges and a place in a field of several hundred, and a place needs every runner drawn together on one shared morning. Quantile trees give seven numbers per runner and no joint draws, so there is nothing to simulate a field from.

So the published distribution is the Bayesian model's, moved. Each runner's draws are multiplied by the single factor that puts their median on the averaged centre, which shifts their whole range with their time and leaves the shared morning intact, so the time and the place a runner reads are the same prediction rather than two. The calibration step then runs on the average's own backtest errors, not the Bayesian model's, which is what keeps the published coverage honest. A first-timer at one of the biggest races is drawn instead from how first-timers actually finished on that course, and is left out of the average: that pool is a measurement, and the trees have nothing to add to it.

How far to lean was chosen on the 2022 and 2023 races only, the same window the challenger's own settings were tuned on, and never on the races scored above. On that window the best weight was {{blend_weight}}, the curve was flat between 0.60 and 0.75, and resampling races put it between 0.50 and 0.80, so what the window fixes is the direction rather than the second decimal. Reading it off the races in the tables above would have made those races report a number about themselves.

For the technical reader: how the backtest avoids cheating

The only way any model sees the archive is through a history object that takes the origin date and keeps nothing dated on or after it, so leakage is something a model cannot reach rather than something to remember; the harness re-checks it at every origin. Two leaks the tests found and closed: races on the same morning are not each other's history, and the runner handed to a model carries only the results the history carries. The model is refitted once per calendar quarter on the history before the quarter, so it knows less about a race late in a quarter than the baselines do, which biases the comparison against it. Errors are reported per history depth with bootstrap intervals, and a baseline that cannot answer for a runner reports nothing rather than falling back to another rule.

Written down before the gun

This is what makes the predictions trustworthy. Each prediction file is saved into the public repository with a fingerprint, and the save is stamped with the time. If a single character were changed later, the fingerprint would no longer match.

  1. Seven days outEvery runner already registered gets a prediction, using that morning's weather forecast.
  2. Each morning afterOnly the runners who registered since are added, so nobody's prediction is published twice.
  3. The day beforeThe whole field is predicted again with the latest forecast, and given places. This is the final file. After it, nothing changes.
  4. Race dayThe gun goes. The system refuses to write a prediction within 24 hours of it.
  5. Results postedThe file is read back from its timestamped record, never from a working copy, and the errors are published beside it.

A mistake found after the gun is scored as it stands and written up, not fixed. That rule is what separates a forecast from a story told afterwards.

For the technical reader: check a prediction yourself

Each file is canonical JSON (sorted keys, fixed precision, UTF-8, LF), committed, and tagged with its SHA-256 in the tag message; the tag time is the record. Git is told never to touch the files' line endings, so the bytes on a Windows checkout match the published hash. To verify one: 

git clone {{repository}}
cd finish-line-forecast
git show predictions/tt-2026            # the tag, its time and the published SHA-256
sha256sum predictions/tt-2026.json      # the same hash, or the file was changed

During a race's week a scheduled task runs each morning at 06:15: it takes the entrant list, writes the day's file with a model fitted once for the week, commits, tags and pushes it, and the website rebuilds itself from the push. Each entrant's random numbers are seeded by their name, not their position on the list, so a runner's prediction is the same whichever morning computes it.

What the data proved wrong

The plan written before any code was reasonable. Measured against the data, {{refuted}} of its designs turned out to be wrong, and every one is logged in the repository with the evidence, so a reader can tell a decision from a guess. A few of them:

"Weather does not matter here"

The first estimate said so. Small races, where the average is mostly noise, were outvoting large ones. Weighted by field size, the effect was clear.

Courses getting harder every year

They were not. The runners were getting older, and without a career trend per runner that ageing was blamed on the road.

The first model lost

Its first backtest was worse than "last time" by 26%. A straight-line career trend predicted years of improvement nobody has. Replaced, it now wins by {{skill}}.

Same name, same town, same person

Runners move. The rule that works is that nobody gets younger.

Eight years missing without a sound

Older index pages used different wording, and the parser found nothing and said nothing. It now fails loudly, and a test holds both spellings.

The recommended sampler

The standard sampler could not fit the model at all. A different one ran the same model cleanly, and the reason is pinned beside the dependency.

One approach is written up in full as rejected: weather as a straight line in temperature, the design most race models use. It looked fine in the headline table, which is why it is the one worth reading. Read it here, or the whole log.

Real people, handled carefully

Every prediction is about a real, named person, so what is published and what is not was decided before anything was built.

  • Only what the results already publish. A name as printed, a hometown as printed, and the prediction. The entrant lists also show shirt sizes; they are never read.
  • No training data, for anyone. The original idea used runners' public Strava activity. Strava's terms forbid displaying other users' data even when it is public, and forbid training models on it, so the public model uses race results only. It is weaker, and the repository says so first.
  • Not in search engines. The prediction data asks search engines not to index it, so a person's prediction does not turn up when someone searches their name.
  • Anyone can opt out. A runner who asks is removed from every future file; the removal is logged without the name. How to ask.
  • No tracking. This page loads nothing from anywhere else: no analytics, no fonts from a third party, no advertising.
  • Ambiguous runners are left out. If the archive cannot tell two people apart, neither gets a published prediction.

For the technical reader: the choices that are easy to get wrong
  • Long runs are checkpointed. A full backtest is eight quarterly model fits of about twenty minutes each on a desktop CPU. Each fit is saved as it finishes, keyed on a hash of the model's source, the data and the settings, so a crashed run resumes and a stale one is never reported.
  • Reports are generated, never typed. The README's tables and this page's numbers are written by the same command from the same objects, so they cannot disagree.
  • Refusals are in code, not in documents. The crawler refuses until the courtesy notes are acknowledged; the freeze refuses inside 24 hours of the gun, with uncommitted code, or without a backtest that matches today's code and data.
  • Time zones are required, not assumed. 08:00 in St. John's is 10:30 UTC, and a naive comparison passes in exactly the wrong direction, so a gun time without a zone is refused.
  • The website has no framework and makes no outside request. Charts are hand-built SVG, and the content security policy forbids anything inline or off-site.

How this was built

Design, methodology, evaluation choices and judgement are Peter Parker's, including years of racing and coaching on these courses: the insight that the Tely's prevailing westerly is a tailwind for almost the whole race, and that the biggest races draw fast visitors the archive has never seen, came from the road before the data confirmed them. AI coding assistants (Claude Code) were used for implementation and drafting, the way a senior engineer uses them in 2026. Every number on this page is reproducible from the repository, and every live prediction is verifiable from a timestamp that predates the race it predicts.

The weather model is the clearest case of that judgement paying off. Four designs failed before it, all of them reasonable: heat as a straight line, humidity, dew point, sunshine as an effect of its own. What worked was Peter's specification, which came from running in it rather than from the data. Heat does not act in a straight line. Below a threshold it costs nothing at all, above it each degree costs more than the last, and it costs more the longer the race, because a 5 km field meets fifteen minutes of weather and a marathon field meets four hours of it. Sunshine has no effect of its own; it raises the temperature a runner feels. Written that way, the weather explains twice as much of what separates one edition of a race from the next as the straight line did.

The arithmetic underneath it is borrowed from Overload, Peter's AI coaching team for runners: Daniels' formulas, the age-grading tables and the heat and wind corrections, including the full-sun figure Overload takes from the US National Weather Service, which this project then measured against eighteen years of races rather than assuming. Constants that arrived from Overload are used as starting assumptions and labelled as such wherever they appear; everything the archive can measure is measured here instead.

Glossary
Backtest
Predicting races that already happened, using only what was known before each one, and scoring the predictions against the results.
Baseline
A simple rule a model must beat to be worth anything, such as "you will run what you ran last time".
Bayesian hierarchical model
A statistical model in which each runner's parameters are estimated together with their group's, so runners with little data borrow strength from runners like them.
Conformal prediction
A way of calibrating prediction ranges on past errors so that they hold as often as they claim, on average, under stated assumptions.
Coverage
How often a range actually contained the outcome. An 80% range should cover about 80% of finishes.
Ensemble, or blend
Averaging the predictions of two models that make different mistakes. Where one is too fast and the other too slow, the average lands closer than either, which is why the published prediction here is an average of two.
Mean absolute error
The average size of a miss, ignoring whether it was too fast or too slow. It describes a pile of predictions, not any one of them: roughly half the misses are smaller than the average and a few are much larger, which is what pulls an average above the middle. For one runner, the range published beside their time is the honest statement of how sure the model is.
Pre-registration
Publishing a prediction, fixed and dated, before the event it predicts, so it cannot be adjusted after the fact.
SHA-256
A fingerprint of a file. Change one character and the fingerprint changes completely.
VDOT
Jack Daniels' single number for running fitness, which puts performances at different distances on one scale.
